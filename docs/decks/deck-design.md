# Deck design — what each deck is for

**Design draft, 2026-10-01.** The determinations below come from a design conversation with the user. They sit above the per-deck sheets in this folder: the sheets list cards, and this file says what those cards are meant to add up to. Where this file and `docs/six-decks-first-cut.md` disagree, this file is the newer decision. The most important difference is that the first-cut doc required every deck to cover every need on its own; that rule is replaced here.

Items marked *(proposal)* are suggestions that have not been agreed yet. Everything else was agreed in conversation.

---

## The goals

1. **Each deck is a different way to play the game.**
2. **Each pair of decks opens up interesting options** that neither deck has alone.
3. **Each deck leaves room for different combinations from game to game**, so two games with the same decks still play differently.

## The rules that follow from them

**Three essentials, many means.** Under the current rules every player needs three things to win: **technology, culture and production.** Every deck must have a route to all three. Everything else is a *means* of progressing, not a requirement: an army, gold, faith, citizens, great people, trade routes, land. A deck uses some of these and not others, and the draft decides which ones a particular game leans on.

**One mechanic per deck.** Each deck is built around a distinct part of the game — Commerce around trade routes, Devotion around religion — not around a yield. A deck still makes many yields, but it makes them *through its mechanic*.

**Each deck has a real gap, and can play around it.** A deck is allowed to be weak at one of the essentials. A second deck can cover that gap, but a deck played on its own should be able to work around it with its own mechanic. The model is Commerce: it is weak at production, so it buys what other empires build. A gap handled this way becomes part of the deck's identity rather than a flaw. Expansionist is the one deck weak at two essentials, science and culture, and it makes up for that by raising the two limits on growth that every other deck runs into: authority and happiness.

**Pairs: mechanics feed each other first, gaps get covered second.** The most interesting pairs are ones where one deck's mechanic creates something the other deck's mechanic can use. Covering each other's gaps is a welcome side effect, not the main point.

**The draft shapes how you play.** It should be hard to force a particular approach from the first turn. Over a full game there should be enough draws to end up with the plan you want, but the route there changes with what you are dealt.

**Each deck must be able to win.** Victory conditions are open to change to make this true. The leader pool is secondary; it can be rearranged to fit the decks once the decks are right.

**Each deck generates its own currency and has its own way to spend it.** This is what a deck's "engine" means: the thing it makes in bulk, and what it turns that into.

| Deck | Generates | Spends it on |
|---|---|---|
| Dominion | Units | Garrisons, raids and conquest |
| Expansionist | Food and land | Citizens (levies) and claimed land |
| Commerce | Gold | Purchases |
| Devotion | Faith | Faith purchases and miracles |
| Scientific | Science | Charter technologies, which unlock the deck's powerful cards |
| Magisterial | Culture | Drafts |

It also gives the pairs a simple test: does one deck's currency feed the other deck's way of spending it?

**Each deck owns a great-person family.** *(proposal)* There are five great-person families, each with its own work, and the prophet's holy site makes a sixth — one per deck. Magisterial also keeps bonuses that apply to great people of *any* family, as its wildcard.

| Family | Its work | Deck |
|---|---|---|
| General | Citadel | Dominion |
| Merchant | Customs house | Commerce |
| Scholar | Academy | Scientific |
| Artist | Landmark | Magisterial |
| Prophet *(a religious unit, not a great-person family)* | Holy site | Devotion |
| Engineer | Manufactory | Expansionist *(proposal)*: public works — aqueducts, irrigation, terraces. The deck already has a hydraulic strand. |

**More chairs: three early, eleven late.** Governments seat **3** cards early in the game and **11** late. The steps between are still to set; today's ladder is 3 / 4 / 6 / 9. Magisterial can seat more than the government allows, through its own abilities.

**Commissions, in every deck.** A commission is a goal card. You seat it, reach its goal using your deck's own mechanic, and it pays a large reward and leaves its chair. Each deck's commissions follow its mechanic: a Dominion commission might be to capture a city, an Expansionist one to tend a number of tiles. Commissions give the draft a target and the game some big moments. They are not a return of the retired feats, quests and endeavours: those were a separate hand dealt to you that earned beads, while a commission is a card you choose to draft and seat.

**Improvements: each deck has a main family, and every pair of decks shares some.** Overlap is how two decks find each other on the map, so some is needed: every pair of decks should share at least some improvement types. But when three or four decks all pay something on the same improvement, it stops belonging to any of them and those cards become generic. Each deck has a main family of its own, and the shared ones are chosen deliberately, pair by pair.

### Two facts about the essentials worth designing around

- **Production is the easiest essential to substitute.** It can be bought with gold, bought with faith, taken by conquest, or hurried by rites. Technology and culture mostly cannot be bought or taken. So production is the natural place for a gap, and a deck that is weak at technology or culture needs a deliberately designed workaround.
- **Culture is also the draft currency.** A deck that is weak at culture sees fewer cards. That is a real cost in a card game, and it can also be an identity: a deck can trade drafts for strength on the map, or the reverse.

## The six decks at a glance

| Deck | The way to play | Core mechanic | Its gap | How it plays around the gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Dominion** | Your army is your economy | Units: training, garrisons, raids, conquest | Developing its own cities, because its production goes into the army | Takes what others built, and its military buildings double as civic ones |
| **Expansionist** | The map is your engine | Raising, tending and claiming land: workers, improvements, tended land, settling, population | **Science and culture** | Gets them only from particular terrain (science from mountains, culture from old forest and wild land), so how much it gets depends on the map; in return it raises authority and happiness, the two limits on growth |
| **Commerce** | Wealth from connection | Trade routes, trading posts, resources, purchasing | Production | Buys what others build, and turns gold into production |
| **Devotion** | Belief as a second economy | Religion: founding, spreading, rites, holy sites, religious units | Production | Spends faith where others spend production: faith purchases and miracles |
| **Scientific** | Reach it first | The tech tree: charter technologies and earlier access | Tempo and raw bonuses | A few science bonuses fund expensive charter technologies, and those unlock the deck's powerful cards |
| **Magisterial** | Play the draft | The card system itself: combinations of specific cards, boons on draft; culture, great people and wonders | Depth in any single engine, and weak until its combinations come together | Boons give it value while it gathers its combinations; conversion turns culture into whatever it needs |

**Where the decks stand.** Dominion and Commerce are in a good place and are the reference for the others. Expansionist, Devotion, Scientific and Magisterial still need work; each section below says where the current sheet falls short of this design.

## Dominion

**The way to play.** Your army is your economy. Training, keeping and using units sustains a working state. Peace, defence, raiding and conquest are different ways of using the same strength.

**Gap and workaround.** Dominion's production goes into its army, so it is weaker at building up its own cities. It plays around that in two ways: its military buildings pay civic yields, and conquest brings in cities other empires have already developed.

**Reaching the three essentials.**
- **Production:** native, though much of it is spent on units. Garrisons and military buildings pay their towns production.
- **Technology:** training and fighting teach. Units trained, enemies killed and military buildings all pay science.
- **Culture:** victories, captures and standing garrisons.

**Bonus themes.**
1. **Garrisons pay their town** — happiness, food, production and culture for units standing in a city.
2. **Training pays** — every unit raised gives something back (faith, food, science, culture).
3. **Fighting pays** — kills and losses give science and culture; barbarian camps are the early-game war.
4. **Military buildings as institutions** — Barracks, War Halls and Armouries give science, culture, gold and building production.
5. **Cheaper armies** — upkeep relief is this deck's version of an economy card.
6. **Conquest pays** — captured cities arrive productive, captures give rewards, and provinces become cheaper to hold.
7. **Unit quality** — mobility, mounted, siege, naval and defensive lines.
8. **War or peace** — cards that pay differently at war and at peace, so a military empire can choose to stay peaceful.
9. **Unit logistics** — moving units around a large empire, mobility, and the ability to field large armies.
10. **Great generals and citadels** — bonuses, access and yields for your empire.
11. **War reparations** — a peace after a won war pays gold or culture, so a war can end in a reward rather than simply stop.

**Where the sheet stands.** The reference deck. It has more self-development than the gap above suggests, but nearly all of it runs through military buildings and garrisons, which is consistent with "the army is the economy".

## Expansionist

**The way to play.** The map is your engine. You raise the land itself — cultivate it, tend it, claim it — and your empire's strength is how good and how extensive its ground is. Expansionist is the deck that raises the two limits on growth every other deck runs into: **authority**, which caps how many towns you can hold, and **happiness**, which caps how big they can grow. It can grow wide, tall, or both.

**Inspiration.** Two traditions of land that looked wild to outsiders but was carefully managed: **pre-Columbian agriculture** (the planted forests of the Amazon, Aztec chinampas, Andean terraces, dark earth) and **Indigenous Australian fire-stick farming** (cultural burning, eel traps, yam-daisy fields). Two Expansionist leaders already come from the first: Pachacuti (Inca terraces) and Nezahualcoyotl (Aztec chinampas).

**Gap and workaround.** Expansionist is weak at **science and culture**: a large body with a small brain. It reaches both **only through particular terrain** — science from mountains, culture from old forest and wild land — so how much it gets depends on where it settles. A mountain start gives a learned empire; a lowland start gives a large, slow one. That dependence on the map is deliberate: it is where this deck gets its variety from game to game. Its natural partners are the decks that supply what it lacks — Scientific and Magisterial — and Dominion, which turns its size into an army.

**Two consequences to accept on purpose.**
- **It drafts slowly.** Culture is the draft currency, so this deck sees fewer cards than the others. It plays the board rather than the card game, but its early cards need to be strong for each card it gets.
- **Authority relief should be earned.** Early settling is already among the strongest things in the game, and a deck that lifts the authority limit feeds that directly. *(proposal)* Towns should become cheaper to hold by developing — working improved or tended land, or connecting to the capital — rather than through flat extra capacity.

**Reaching the three essentials.**
- **Production:** worked land — hills, mines, forests, chopping, pastures.
- **Technology:** weak. Only from terrain that teaches: mountains above all.
- **Culture:** weak. Only from terrain that inspires: old forest, wild land, coast.

**Bonus themes.**
1. **Terrain has a job.** Each kind of land pays something specific, and this is how the deck reaches science and culture. For example: mountains give science; rivers and lakes give food and happiness; hills give production and defence; old forest and wild land give culture; desert and tundra get a reason to be settled at all.
2. **Raising the land.** Adding yields to individual tiles is the deck's own objective: over a game an Expansionist player builds up its best tiles, and every game's best tiles are different. *(proposal)* Possible forms: **dark earth** (land gets richer the longer it is worked); **the Three Sisters** (a farm gains from particular neighbours, such as a plantation or a pasture beside it — a specific pairing, not a count of kinds); terraces and chinampas that enrich the ones beside them.
3. **Tended land** — the managed wild. See below.
4. **Authority from developed land.** How the deck grows wide: towns that work their land, or connect to the capital, cost less to hold.
5. **Happiness from land and food.** How the deck grows tall: well-fed, well-watered cities are content. *(proposal)* Wild land as a commons — wild land inside your borders makes your cities content.
6. **Territory gain and management** — getting ground without waiting for borders to grow, and getting the most out of the ground you hold, through improvements and tending. Magisterial shares this theme from a different direction (great-person works), which makes territory one of the places the two decks meet.
7. **Population as a resource** — growth pays, and citizens can be spent (levies) for production or units.
8. **Settlement** — founding and connecting towns, with established towns supporting new ones.
9. **Engineers and public works** *(proposal)* — the engineer and its manufactory, as the great-person family this deck owns: aqueducts, irrigation and terraces.

### Tended land

**What it is.** Light-touch works that add yields to a tile but leave the land wild. They are the deck's own family of works, separate from ordinary improvements.

**Agreed.**
- The keyword is **tended**: you *tend* a tile, and the result is *tended land*.
- A tended tile is **not an improvement**. It still counts as wild land for the deck's wild-land bonuses (The Old Songs, the groves, the commons), so tending and the wild-land line work together.
- **Tended land cannot be pillaged.**
- Tended works are **unlocked by Expansionist cards**, so the managed wild belongs to this deck.

**The works.** From the two traditions:
- **Forest garden** (the planted Amazon): on forest or jungle; food and production; the forest stays standing.
- **Fish traps** (the Budj Bim eel traps in Victoria): on rivers and lakeshores.
- **Root-crop grounds** (the murnong yam-daisy fields of south-east Australia): on grassland.
- **Burned country** (fire-stick farming): grassland and scrub that has been burned, with more food and game.
- **Wild orchards** (nut and fruit groves): on hills or forest; food and gold.
- **Fertilising.**

**Proposals.**
- *(proposal)* **Tended works yield less than a proper improvement** — a farm still beats a garden on raw food — **and do not open resources**, so a luxury or strategic tile still pulls toward a proper improvement. This keeps the choice real and gives the deck **two ways to farm**: *the field* (farms, pastures, terraces) and *the managed wild* (tended land plus the wild-land bonuses). The draft decides which one a game leans into.
- *(proposal)* **Workers decide where, once; towns decide when, on a cycle.** A worker's tending is a single action that pays forever. Anything that recurs — burning above all — is one decision per town, never one per tile: a town declares a **burning season**, burns all its scrub and grassland at once, yields less for a few turns and more for a long stretch, and cannot burn again for a while. The decision is *when* to burn, not *where*. This follows the ruling on augurs, which became a town action because a unit walked to each town was too many clicks for too little.
- *(proposal)* **Fertilising is soil, not a work.** It sits underneath whatever is on the tile — a farm included — and survives when that work is pillaged or replaced, the way Amazonian dark earth is still fertile centuries later. Both ways of farming can then use it, and it becomes the part of the deck's investment that lasts.
- *(proposal)* **Three words for three states:** *improved* (an ordinary improvement), *tended* (a tended work), and *wild* (no improvement — untouched or tended). The wild-land cards would say "wild" instead of "unimproved", so the rule reads *tended land is still wild* rather than *tended land counts as unimproved*. The risk: some flavour text uses "the wild" to mean barbarians. The fallback is to keep "unimproved" in rules text.

### Ideas not yet chosen

- **Outposts** — a worker plants a claim that owns the land around it, with no town and no authority cost; it can later grow into a town.
- **Prospecting** — the buried-ore system and the worker's prospect action exist, and no card touches them; Expansionist could find, and profit from, more seams.
- **The breadbasket** — surplus food turns into something useful once a city has stopped growing: happiness, authority, settlers.
- **Daughter towns** — a town settled from another town costs no authority at first and sends food back to its parent.
- **Hinterland** — big cities work one ring further out.
- **The barbarian frontier** — clearing a camp claims the land around it.
- **Migration** — citizens drift from crowded towns to small ones with room.
- **Reshaping the land** — workers change terrain itself. The most expensive idea: terrain cannot change mid-game today, by a deliberate rule.

**Avoid.**
- **Bonuses that count how many different kinds of terrain or feature you have.** Terrain-*conditional* bonuses are good; counting kinds is not.
- **Science or culture that does not come from terrain.** It covers the gap directly instead of playing around it.

**Where the sheet stands.**
- Cultivation is well covered.
- **Authority and happiness are almost entirely unbuilt.** Of the deck's own cards, only The Grain Dole (happiness for large cities) is live. The authority cards — The Local Census, Provincial Councils, The Pioneers' Portion, The Standing Patronage — are all deferred.
- **Tended land does not exist yet.** It is a new family of works and needs building.
- **Claiming is thin.** Claim by the Spade, the deck's central claiming card, is deferred.
- **The woodland cycle and levies are mostly deferred**, because planting and spending citizens do not exist in the game yet.
- **Science, against the new gap.** The Highlands (science on hill mines and quarries) is terrain-conditional and fits. The Naturalists (science per *kind* of feature worked) counts kinds and does not. The Growing Annals and The Provincial Schools (both deferred) and the shared Hydraulic Schools (live) give science that does not come from terrain.
- **Culture, against the new gap.** The Old Songs (wild land), The Coast (fishing boats) and Forest Sanctuaries (old forest, deferred) are terrain-conditional and fit. Charcoal Burners pays culture from chopping, which spends the land rather than reading it.

## Commerce

**The way to play.** Wealth from connection. You connect places to each other and use the wealth that creates to act where others must wait.

**Gap and workaround.** Weak at production. It buys what others build, and converts gold into production.

**Reaching the three essentials.**
- **Production:** purchasing, and gold turned into production.
- **Technology:** foreign trade routes, gold buildings, strategic resources and mines.
- **Culture:** foreign trade routes, trading posts, and purchases.

**Bonus themes.**

*Making gold*
1. **The gold engine** — mines, luxuries and strategic resources pay gold; rewards for holding many different kinds and for extra copies.
2. **Luxury resource yields** — luxuries paying more than their base.
3. **Improvements** — main family: mines, quarries, plantations and fishing boats (extraction and cash crops).
4. **Monopolies** — a reward for holding most of the copies of a luxury.
5. **Gold buildings as institutions** — markets and banks pay the way Dominion's military buildings do.

*Trade*

6. **Domestic trade routes** — routes move food and production between your own towns.
7. **Foreign and sea trade routes** — science and culture from abroad; range and distance.
8. **Trading posts and roads** — a lasting presence in a town, at home or abroad, and the roads between.
9. **Caravanserais.**
10. **Tolls** — income from foreign trade routes that cross your land.
11. **Exploration and discovery** — finding new lands, peoples and ruins. This theme belongs to Commerce. Other decks may share it for cross-deck synergy, but it should not be strong in Scientific.

*Spending*

12. **Purchasing and gold's flexibility** — discounts, and new things that can be bought: units, buildings, tiles, wonders, great people, draft rerolls.
13. **Gold converted** — gold turned into production, science or culture.
14. **Reserves** — the treasury as an asset in its own right.

*People and partners*

15. **Great merchants and customs houses.**
16. **Diplomacy and mutual bonuses** — deals and partnerships that pay both sides.

**Where the sheet stands.** The reference deck, and the user's hand-edited sheet is the design of record for its cards.

## Devotion

**The way to play.** Belief as a second economy. Faith is a currency you earn from your land and practices and spend in place of production, and your belief spreads past your borders.

**Gap and workaround.** Weak at production. It spends faith where others spend production: buying units, workers, buildings and tiles with faith, and spending faith on immediate effects (miracles) such as healing units or growing a city.

**Reaching the three essentials.**
- **Production:** faith purchases and miracles, not flat production bonuses.
- **Culture:** religious buildings, holy sites and festivals.
- **Technology:** religious learning (a Temple beside a Library), and knowledge paid by cities that follow your religion.

**Bonus themes.**
1. **The faith engine** — from pastures, holy sites, shrines and observances.
2. **Spending faith** — faith purchases and miracles. This is the production workaround and should be the core of the deck.
3. **Rites as decisions** — rites with real trade-offs, rather than an on-switch that turns on city bonuses.
4. **Spreading belief** — pressure, proclamations and missionaries; rewards for foreign cities that follow you.
5. **Sacred places** — holy sites, relics and cathedrals as centres.
6. **Pilgrimage** — trade routes to holy places.
7. **Holy war** — an optional military strand that fights best in its religion's territory.
8. **Beliefs amplified** — bonuses per belief held, and cards that strengthen your religion's own beliefs.
9. **Early investment, ramping bonuses** — faith put in early pays back more as the game goes on.
10. **Improvements** — main family: pastures and camps (herds and hunting grounds), plus holy sites. Mines, quarries and plantations are Commerce's main family; which of them Devotion shares is settled in the pairs pass.
11. **Happiness from faith** — religion keeps people content.
12. **Religious units** — prophets, apostles and inquisitors, and their charges.
13. **Defending your faith** — resisting conversion, and the inquisition.

**Avoid.** Many flat "+production in cities keeping a rite" bonuses. They cover the gap directly instead of playing around it, and they turn rites into a switch rather than a choice.

**Where the sheet stands.**
- **"Cities keeping a rite gain…" is overused.** About ten cards use it as a condition for flat yields (Monastic Table, Votive Fields, Omen Schools, Rites of Labor, The Rites Commonwealth, The Festival Calendar, The Consecrated Workshops, and others). Several of these give production, which works against the gap.
- **The faith-spending workaround is mostly deferred.** Consecrated Labor, The Votive Companies, Consecrated Ground, The Healing and The Loaves are all unbuilt. The deck's defining mechanic is the part that does not exist yet.
- Conversion rewards (The Living Hymnal, The Congregation Rolls, The Great Consecration) and holy war (The Spiritual Host, The Holy Army) are in place.

## Scientific

**The way to play.** Reach it first. Lower tempo and fewer bonuses than the other decks, but you get things earlier than anyone else, or things nobody else can have.

**Gap and workaround.** Weak at tempo and raw bonuses. What it fields is a step ahead of what anyone else can field.

**Balance note from the user.** Science is probably the strongest yield, because technology already carries military power and economic bonuses. The "fewer bonuses, lower tempo" cost has to be real, or this becomes the best deck.

**How science is tapered: charter technologies.** Scientific has a few science bonuses — enough to be a science engine — and the taper is that its powerful bonuses sit behind **charter technologies**, which cost more research than ordinary technologies. A Scientific game has to invest its science in charters to reach the strongest cards in its pool. So the science the deck generates is spent on access, not simply on getting through the ordinary tree faster.

**How charters work.**
- **Charters keep their current behaviour:** a charter is drafted, never sits in a chair, and grants its unlock the moment it is picked. The user (2026-10-06), reversing a proposal to require the charter to be seated while its technology is researched: *"keep the old behavior for charters, i want to make the game as fluid/not tedious as possible."*
- Charter technologies cost more research than ordinary technologies at the same point in the tree. This is the taper.
- **Charter technologies get their own section in the tech tree.** The interface work for that is scoped for later; this is the plan it builds toward.

**Reaching the three essentials.**
- **Technology:** native, through a few science bonuses and through charters.
- **Production:** from what it unlocks early — better buildings and units sooner — and from institutions.
- **Culture:** from being first to discoveries, and from institutions that mature.

**Bonus themes.**
1. **The science engine** — a few science bonuses, kept small because charters are where the science goes.
2. **Charter technologies, improvements, units and buildings** — research paths other empires cannot take, and what those paths unlock.
3. **Early access** — units, buildings or improvements available before their normal age, or versions nobody else can build.
4. **Being first** — rewards for reaching a technology before any other empire.
5. **Institutions that mature** — science buildings and empire buildings that pay more the longer they stand. This is where the low tempo is paid for.
6. **Strategic resources** — access to, use of, and bonuses for strategic resources.
7. **Applied knowledge** — discoveries turned into production and construction.
8. **Scholars and academies** — the great-person family this deck owns.
9. **Scaling from buildings and population.**
10. **Happiness synergies.**
11. **Libraries and universities** — synergies between the deck's core science buildings.

**Avoid.**
- More than a few direct science bonuses; charters are where the deck's strength should come from.
- Exploration and discovery as a strong theme. It belongs to Commerce; a light version here is fine for cross-deck synergy.
- Great-person cards beyond scholars and academies. Other families belong to other decks, and to Magisterial's any-family bonuses.

**Where the sheet stands.**
- **The "access" identity is almost entirely unbuilt.** All five research charters are deferred because the technologies they open do not exist yet, and The Second Seat (an extra copy of an empire building) is deferred.
- **The live cards lean on great-person works and maturity.** Maturity fits the design well (The Long Study, The Old Academy, Enduring Institutions, The Long Inheritance). The great-person works cards (Working Estates, The Endowed Provinces, Master Artificers, The Quiet Faculties) cover families other than scholars and should narrow to academies or move.
- **Exploration and discovery cards** (Field Notebooks, Natural Philosophy, Practical Astronomy) belong to Commerce. A light version can stay here for cross-deck synergy.

## Magisterial

**The way to play.** Play the draft. Magisterial is meant to be the most unusual of the six decks: it leans into the drafting side of the game, and its flexibility comes through drafting rather than through yields. If Expansionist plays the board, Magisterial plays the card game.

**Two things that set it apart.**
- **Its power comes from combinations of cards.** More than any other deck, its strongest effects need several cards working together. A single Magisterial card is modest; the right combination of them is strong.
- **It has more boons than any other deck.** Cards that pay something when drafted ("on draft, gain…"). Boons give the deck value while it gathers its combinations.

### How its combinations work

**Colours are the sets.** Magisterial cards carry a colour, and a colour is a set: holding several cards of one colour is what unlocks their stronger effects. Sets are kept small, because a small set is one a player can read and track. *Still open:* how many colours, how many cards each, whether colours appear only on Magisterial cards or across all decks, and how a colour shows on the card face (see open questions).

**Draft actions: cards that let you act, and cards that pay you for acting.** Combinations form around the things a player *does* in the draft — drafting, passing, rerolling, seating and unseating — rather than around cards that name each other. One card lets you take an action more often or more cheaply; another pays you each time you take it. The user's example, not yet a decision: *"gain 1 free re-roll on every draft"* beside *"gives +1 science and +1 culture for each time you've re-rolled."* These combine without either card naming the other, and the action they share is visible to the player.

**Cards outside the government count.** Magisterial has synergies for cards that are **not seated** — the rest of your collection — and for **cards you passed**. So its combinations do not all have to fit in the government, and passing on a hand becomes a real Magisterial play rather than only a fallback.

**The archive is a card ability:** a card that lets you keep one card from a hand you pass.

**Magisterial can seat more cards** than its government allows, through its own abilities.

**Rejected for this deck:** cards that name a specific partner card; cards that affect their neighbours in the government; two cards fusing into one; cards that copy another card's effect.

**Gap and workaround.** No single engine is especially deep, and it is weak until its combinations come together. Boons carry it while it gathers them, and conversion turns its culture into whatever it needs.

**Reaching the three essentials.**
- **Culture:** native. Culture is also the draft currency, so this deck sees more cards.
- **Production and technology:** by converting culture, through great people of any family, and through wonders.

**Bonus themes.**
1. **The culture engine.**
2. **Colour sets and draft actions** — see "How its combinations work" above.
3. **Boons on draft** — paying something when a card is drafted. Including **random boons that are strong in total**: individually unpredictable, reliably valuable over a game. For example: on draft, gain a helpful card from any deck's pool (the game can already price a card a seat has not drafted, because the bot does it every draft); on draft, gain a random great person.
4. **Bending the card system** — rerolls, expanded governments, moving cards between chairs, choosing what a card pays, more choice in a draft.
5. **Conversion** — culture turned into production, science, gold or food.
6. **Great people as wildcards** — bonuses that apply to any family, with the choice of an act, a work or a legacy; renown from many sources.
7. **Great-person works and wonders** — bonuses toward building great-person improvements and wonders; completing a wonder as a moment that pays out across the empire.
8. **Territory gain and management through great-person works** — great-person improvements claiming and shaping land, possibly through specific **arrangements** of improvements. Expansionist shares this theme through its own improvements and tending.
9. **Culture buildings as the base** — as the deck's culture engine, not as a gate for flat bonuses.
10. **Breadth rewards** — paying for holding many *different* things: building categories, great-person families, kinds of work.

**Avoid.** Many "cities with at least two (or three) culture buildings gain flat yields" cards. That is the stacking-bonus pattern this redesign is meant to move away from.

**Where the sheet stands.**
- The most complete deck, but most of it is building-count conditions (a Monument, two culture buildings, three culture buildings) paying flat yields.
- **The conversion theme already has seeds:** The Working Court, The Learned Patron, The Performers' Treasury and The Universal Court all turn culture into another yield.
- **Breadth rewards have seeds too:** The Complete Court and The Living Canon.
- **Nothing touches the card system yet, and no card depends on another specific card.** The deck's two defining features — combinations and boons — barely exist.
- Its great-person improvement cards (The Groundskeepers, The Laureate, The Masters' Domain, The Sculptors' Schools) apply to every family, which fits Magisterial's any-family role.

## What pairs of decks should enable

*To be designed together.* The principle: in a good pair, one deck's mechanic creates something the other deck's mechanic can use. Covering each other's gaps is a bonus.

Two examples from the conversation:
- **Devotion and Dominion:** cities converted to your religion become targets your army is strong against, and a holy war spreads the faith as it goes.
- **Commerce and Dominion:** Commerce buys the buildings Dominion has no production to spare for, and Dominion protects the trade routes Commerce depends on.

## Open questions

1. **Expansionist's tended land.** The open proposals are listed in its section: whether tended works yield less than improvements and open no resources; whether burning is a town's season rather than a worker's action; whether fertilising is soil or a tended work; and whether "wild" replaces "unimproved" in rules text.
2. **Great-person families.** The proposal in the rules section gives each deck one family and Magisterial the any-family bonuses. Still to confirm: the table as a whole, and whether the engineer goes to Expansionist as public works.
3. **Magisterial's colours.** How many colours, and how many cards in each; whether colours appear only on Magisterial cards or on every deck's cards; and how a colour shows on the card face, since the frame and emblem already carry the deck's own colour.
4. **Devotion and Commerce share a gap.** Both are weak at production; one substitutes gold and the other faith. Is that distinct enough, or should one of them have a different gap?
5. **Victory conditions.** Each deck must be able to win. Expansionist and Devotion currently have no wager family of their own to earn beads from. Victory conditions are open to change to fix this. *(proposal)* For Expansionist: **cultivate a heartland** — hold a number of tiles raised to their richest level. It grows out of what the deck already does, only this deck pursues it, and it cannot be finished quickly.
6. **Which improvements each pair shares.** Every pair of decks should share at least some improvement types, chosen deliberately. Settled in the pairs pass.
