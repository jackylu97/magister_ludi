# Deck design — what each deck is for

**Design draft, 2026-10-01.** The determinations below come from a design conversation with the user. They sit above the per-deck sheets in this folder: the sheets list cards, and this file says what those cards are meant to add up to. Where this file and `docs/six-decks-first-cut.md` disagree, this file is the newer decision. The most important difference is that the first-cut doc required every deck to cover every need on its own; that rule is replaced here.

Items marked *(proposal)* are suggestions that have not been agreed yet. Everything else was agreed in conversation.

---

## The goals

1. **Each deck is a different way to play the game.**
2. **Each pair of decks opens up interesting options** that neither deck has alone.
3. **Each deck leaves room for different combinations from game to game**, so two games with the same decks still play differently.

## The rules that follow from them

**Three essentials, many means.** Under the current rules every player needs three things to win: **technology, culture and production.** Every deck must have a route to all three. Everything else is a *means* of progressing, not a requirement: an army, gold, faith, citizens, great people, trade routes, land. A deck uses some of these and not others, and the draft decides which ones a particular game leans on.

**One mechanic per deck.** Each deck is built around a distinct part of the game — Commerce around trade routes, Devotion around religion — not around a yield. A deck still makes many yields, but it makes them *through its mechanic*.

**Each deck has a real gap, and can play around it.** A deck is allowed to be weak at one of the essentials. A second deck can cover that gap, but a deck played on its own should be able to work around it with its own mechanic. The model is Commerce: it is weak at production, so it buys what other empires build. A gap handled this way becomes part of the deck's identity rather than a flaw.

**Pairs: mechanics feed each other first, gaps get covered second.** The most interesting pairs are ones where one deck's mechanic creates something the other deck's mechanic can use. Covering each other's gaps is a welcome side effect, not the main point.

**The draft shapes how you play.** It should be hard to force a particular approach from the first turn. Over a full game there should be enough draws to end up with the plan you want, but the route there changes with what you are dealt.

**Each deck must be able to win.** Victory conditions are open to change to make this true. The leader pool is secondary; it can be rearranged to fit the decks once the decks are right.

### Two facts about the essentials worth designing around

- **Production is the easiest essential to substitute.** It can be bought with gold, bought with faith, taken by conquest, or hurried by rites. Technology and culture mostly cannot be bought or taken. So production is the natural place for a gap, and a deck that is weak at technology or culture needs a deliberately designed workaround.
- **Culture is also the draft currency.** A deck that is weak at culture sees fewer cards. That is a real cost in a card game, and it can also be an identity: a deck can trade drafts for strength on the map, or the reverse.

## The six decks at a glance

| Deck | The way to play | Core mechanic | Its gap | How it plays around the gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Dominion** | Your army is your economy | Units: training, garrisons, raids, conquest | Developing its own cities, because its production goes into the army | Takes what others built, and its military buildings double as civic ones |
| **Expansionist** | The map is your engine | Cultivating and claiming land: workers, improvements, settling, population | **Science** | The land teaches you: science from working and discovering varied land, not from science buildings *(proposal)* |
| **Commerce** | Wealth from connection | Trade routes, trading posts, resources, purchasing | Production | Buys what others build, and turns gold into production |
| **Devotion** | Belief as a second economy | Religion: founding, spreading, rites, holy sites, religious units | Production | Spends faith where others spend production: faith purchases and miracles |
| **Scientific** | Reach it first | The tech tree: earlier and exclusive access | Tempo and raw bonuses | What it fields is a step ahead of what anyone else can field |
| **Magisterial** | Adapt and redirect | Culture, great people, wonders, and the card system itself | Depth in any single engine | Converts one thing into another and bends the card system to what it needs now |

**Where the decks stand.** Dominion and Commerce are in a good place and are the reference for the others. Expansionist, Devotion, Scientific and Magisterial still need work; each section below says where the current sheet falls short of this design.

## Dominion

**The way to play.** Your army is your economy. Training, keeping and using units sustains a working state. Peace, defence, raiding and conquest are different ways of using the same strength.

**Gap and workaround.** Dominion's production goes into its army, so it is weaker at building up its own cities. It plays around that in two ways: its military buildings pay civic yields, and conquest brings in cities other empires have already developed.

**Reaching the three essentials.**
- **Production:** native, though much of it is spent on units. Garrisons and military buildings pay their towns production.
- **Technology:** training and fighting teach. Units trained, enemies killed and military buildings all pay science.
- **Culture:** victories, captures and standing garrisons.

**Bonus themes.**
1. **Garrisons pay their town** — happiness, food, production and culture for units standing in a city.
2. **Training pays** — every unit raised gives something back (faith, food, science, culture).
3. **Fighting pays** — kills and losses give science and culture; barbarian camps are the early-game war.
4. **Military buildings as institutions** — Barracks, War Halls and Armouries give science, culture, gold and building production.
5. **Cheaper armies** — upkeep relief is this deck's version of an economy card.
6. **Conquest pays** — captured cities arrive productive, captures give rewards, and provinces become cheaper to hold.
7. **Unit quality** — mobility, mounted, siege, naval and defensive lines.
8. **War or peace** — cards that pay differently at war and at peace, so a military empire can choose to stay peaceful.

**Where the sheet stands.** The reference deck. It has more self-development than the gap above suggests, but nearly all of it runs through military buildings and garrisons, which is consistent with "the army is the economy".

## Expansionist

**The way to play.** The map is your engine. You cultivate land well and claim more of it, and your empire's strength is the quality and extent of the ground it works.

**Gap and workaround.** Expansionist is weak at **science**. Science is the hardest essential to substitute, so the workaround has to be designed. *(proposal)* **The land teaches you:** science comes from working and discovering a variety of land — different terrains and features worked, new kinds of ground found — and from population itself, rather than from science buildings. Slow and steady, with a lower ceiling than Scientific, and it does not step on Scientific's identity.

**Natural partners.** Culture and faith. Wild land, old groves, sacred sites and settled traditions give an Expansionist empire culture and faith without needing a second deck.

**Reaching the three essentials.**
- **Production:** worked land — hills, mines, forests, chopping, pastures.
- **Culture:** wild and worked land, harvest traditions, and a growing population. Claiming land directly also means it needs less culture for borders.
- **Technology:** the weak one. Comes through the "land teaches you" workaround above.

**Bonus themes.**
1. **Worked land pays more** — farms, pastures, mines and camps, plus regional specialities (highlands, steppe, coast, floodplain).
2. **Claiming land** — getting ground without waiting for borders to grow: workers claiming hexes beside the border, settling, buying or taking tiles. This is half of the deck's identity and should be one of its biggest themes.
3. **The woodland cycle** — keep forest, plant it, chop it, let it regrow. A running choice between keeping land wild and harvesting it.
4. **Wild land is sacred** — unimproved land and forests give culture and faith.
5. **Population as a resource** — growth pays, and citizens can be spent (levies) for production or units.
6. **Variety of land as knowledge** — science from the number of different terrains and features worked or discovered. This is the science workaround. *(proposal)*
7. **Settlement** — founding and connecting towns, with established towns supporting new ones.

**Avoid.** Flat science bonuses on improvements or buildings. They cover the gap directly instead of playing around it.

**Where the sheet stands.**
- Cultivation is well covered.
- **Claiming is thin.** Claim by the Spade, the deck's central claiming card, is deferred, and little else claims land. Half the deck's identity is mostly missing.
- **The woodland cycle and levies are mostly deferred**, because planting and population-spending do not exist in the game yet.
- **Several live cards give science directly**, which now conflicts with the gap: The Highlands (+2 science on hill mines), The Growing Annals, The Provincial Schools, and the shared Hydraulic Schools. The Naturalists (science per distinct feature worked) is the right kind of science for this deck and is the model for the workaround.

## Commerce

**The way to play.** Wealth from connection. You connect places to each other and use the wealth that creates to act where others must wait.

**Gap and workaround.** Weak at production. It buys what others build, and converts gold into production.

**Reaching the three essentials.**
- **Production:** purchasing, and gold turned into production.
- **Technology:** foreign trade routes, gold buildings, strategic resources and mines.
- **Culture:** foreign trade routes, trading posts, and purchases.

**Bonus themes.**
1. **Resource wealth** — mines, luxuries and strategic resources pay gold; rewards for holding many different kinds and for extra copies.
2. **Domestic trade routes** — routes move food and production between your own towns.
3. **Foreign and sea trade routes** — science and culture from abroad; range and distance.
4. **Trading posts** — a lasting presence in a town, at home or abroad.
5. **Purchasing** — discounts, and new things that can be bought: units, buildings, tiles, wonders, great people, draft rerolls.
6. **Gold converted** — gold turned into production, science or culture.
7. **Reserves** — the treasury as an asset in its own right.

**Where the sheet stands.** The reference deck, and the user's hand-edited sheet is the design of record for its cards.

## Devotion

**The way to play.** Belief as a second economy. Faith is a currency you earn from your land and practices and spend in place of production, and your belief spreads past your borders.

**Gap and workaround.** Weak at production. It spends faith where others spend production: buying units, workers, buildings and tiles with faith, and spending faith on immediate effects (miracles) such as healing units or growing a city.

**Reaching the three essentials.**
- **Production:** faith purchases and miracles, not flat production bonuses.
- **Culture:** religious buildings, holy sites and festivals.
- **Technology:** religious learning (a Temple beside a Library), and knowledge paid by cities that follow your religion.

**Bonus themes.**
1. **Faith income** — from pastures, holy sites, shrines and observances.
2. **Spending faith** — faith purchases and miracles. This is the production workaround and should be the core of the deck.
3. **Rites as decisions** — rites with real trade-offs, rather than an on-switch that turns on city bonuses.
4. **Spreading belief** — pressure, proclamations and missionaries; rewards for foreign cities that follow you.
5. **Sacred places** — holy sites, relics and cathedrals as centres.
6. **Pilgrimage** — trade routes to holy places.
7. **Holy war** — an optional military strand that fights best in its religion's territory.

**Avoid.** Many flat "+production in cities keeping a rite" bonuses. They cover the gap directly instead of playing around it, and they turn rites into a switch rather than a choice.

**Where the sheet stands.**
- **"Cities keeping a rite gain…" is overused.** About ten cards use it as a condition for flat yields (Monastic Table, Votive Fields, Omen Schools, Rites of Labor, The Rites Commonwealth, The Festival Calendar, The Consecrated Workshops, and others). Several of these give production, which works against the gap.
- **The faith-spending workaround is mostly deferred.** Consecrated Labor, The Votive Companies, Consecrated Ground, The Healing and The Loaves are all unbuilt. The deck's defining mechanic is the part that does not exist yet.
- Conversion rewards (The Living Hymnal, The Congregation Rolls, The Great Consecration) and holy war (The Spiritual Host, The Holy Army) are in place.

## Scientific

**The way to play.** Reach it first. Lower tempo and fewer bonuses than the other decks, but you get things earlier than anyone else, or things nobody else can have.

**Gap and workaround.** Weak at tempo and raw bonuses. What it fields is a step ahead of what anyone else can field.

**Balance note from the user.** Science is probably the strongest yield, because technology already carries military power and economic bonuses. Direct research boosts should be a small part of this deck. The "fewer bonuses, lower tempo" cost has to be real, or this becomes the best deck.

**Reaching the three essentials.**
- **Technology:** native, but through access rather than large research bonuses.
- **Production:** from what it unlocks early — better buildings and units sooner — and from institutions.
- **Culture:** from being first to discoveries, and from institutions that mature.

**Bonus themes.**
1. **Charters** — research paths other empires cannot take.
2. **Early access** — units, buildings or improvements available before their normal age, or versions nobody else can build.
3. **Being first** — rewards for reaching a technology before any other empire.
4. **Institutions that mature** — science buildings and empire buildings that pay more the longer they stand. This is where the low tempo is paid for.
5. **Strategic capability** — access to and use of strategic resources.
6. **Applied knowledge** — discoveries turned into production and construction.

**Avoid.** Large flat science bonuses, and heavy overlap with Magisterial on great people (see open questions).

**Where the sheet stands.**
- **The "access" identity is almost entirely unbuilt.** All five research charters are deferred because the technologies they open do not exist yet, and The Second Seat (an extra copy of an empire building) is deferred.
- **The live cards lean on great-person works and maturity.** Maturity fits the design well (The Long Study, The Old Academy, Enduring Institutions, The Long Inheritance). The great-person works cards (Working Estates, The Endowed Provinces, Master Artificers, The Quiet Faculties) overlap heavily with Magisterial.
- Exploration and discovery cards (Field Notebooks, Natural Philosophy, Practical Astronomy) may belong partly to Expansionist under the "land teaches you" proposal.

## Magisterial

**The way to play.** Adapt and redirect. Flexible, interesting bonuses: turning one thing into another, and bending the card system to what you need this game. If Expansionist plays the board, Magisterial plays the card game.

**Gap and workaround.** No single engine is especially deep. It plays around that by converting and redirecting.

**Reaching the three essentials.**
- **Culture:** native. Culture is also the draft currency, so this deck sees more cards.
- **Production and technology:** by converting culture, through great people of any family, and through wonders.

**Bonus themes.**
1. **Conversion** — culture turned into production, science, gold or food. This is the core of the deck's flexibility.
2. **Bending the card system** *(proposal)* — moving cards between chairs freely, choosing what a card pays, more choice in a draft. Card *selection*, not card *advantage*: more say over which cards you use, not simply more cards.
3. **Great people as wildcards** — any family, with the choice of an act, a work or a legacy; renown from many sources.
4. **Wonders as bursts** — completing a wonder as a moment that pays out across the empire.
5. **Culture buildings as the base** — as the deck's culture engine, not as a gate for flat bonuses.
6. **Breadth rewards** — paying for holding many *different* things: building categories, great-person families, kinds of work.

**Avoid.** Many "cities with at least two (or three) culture buildings gain flat yields" cards. That is the stacking-bonus pattern this redesign is meant to move away from.

**Where the sheet stands.**
- The most complete deck, but most of it is building-count conditions (a Monument, two culture buildings, three culture buildings) paying flat yields.
- **The conversion theme already has seeds:** The Working Court, The Learned Patron, The Performers' Treasury and The Universal Court all turn culture into another yield.
- **Breadth rewards have seeds too:** The Complete Court and The Living Canon.
- **Nothing touches the card system yet.**
- Its great-person improvement cards (The Groundskeepers, The Laureate, The Masters' Domain, The Sculptors' Schools) overlap with Scientific's.

## What pairs of decks should enable

*To be designed together.* The principle: in a good pair, one deck's mechanic creates something the other deck's mechanic can use. Covering each other's gaps is a bonus.

Two examples from the conversation:
- **Devotion and Dominion:** cities converted to your religion become targets your army is strong against, and a holy war spreads the faith as it goes.
- **Commerce and Dominion:** Commerce buys the buildings Dominion has no production to spare for, and Dominion protects the trade routes Commerce depends on.

## Open questions

1. **Expansionist's science workaround.** Is "the land teaches you" the right idea, and if so, do the discovery cards currently in Scientific move to Expansionist?
2. **Who owns great people?** Both Scientific and Magisterial have a large great-person strand. One option: Magisterial treats great people as flexible wildcards (any family, any use), while Scientific uses only the works that open new capability (academies, manufactories). Another is to give great people to one deck outright.
3. **Magisterial and the card system.** Is bending the card system (reslotting, choosing what a card pays, more choice in a draft) the right form for Magisterial's flexibility?
4. **Devotion and Commerce share a gap.** Both are weak at production; one substitutes gold and the other faith. Is that distinct enough, or should one of them have a different gap?
5. **Victory conditions.** Each deck must be able to win. Expansionist and Devotion currently have no wager family of their own to earn beads from. Victory conditions are open to change to fix this.
